# 04. DocuSharePro Response

Given the suspicious consent activity affecting 112 users, I would treat DocuSharePro as a suspected OAuth consent phishing incident and escalate to Security.

## Step 1: Containment

**Action:** Disable DocuSharePro and revoke its existing consent grants for all 112 users.

**Why first:** Remove the application's authorisation at the directory level before revoking user sessions. Otherwise, the application remains authorised and could obtain new tokens.

- Stops: New consent approvals and token issuance.
- Still exposed: Previously issued access tokens may remain valid until expiry or invalidation. Refresh tokens granted through "stay signed in" (offline access) must also be addressed. Any data already accessed or modified remains an investigation concern.
  
<BR>

## Step 2: Session Revocation

**Action:** Revoke active sign-in sessions for all 112 affected users.

**Why second:** With DocuSharePro's authorisation removed, session revocation addresses the remaining token persistence risk.

- Stops: Existing refresh tokens, including those associated with offline access, from obtaining new access tokens once revocation takes effect.
- Still exposed: Previously issued access tokens may remain valid until expiry or invalidation.

  <BR>

 ## Step 3: Investigation

**Action:** Work with the Incident Response team to investigate how users granted consent and review the 112 affected accounts for suspicious activity.

Why third: The priority is to stop further exposure before investigating what has already happened, while preserving evidence from the start.  

- Establishes: How users encountered the app, whether its permissions were misused and what data may have been accessed or modified. I'd start with the six users who granted consent before the weekend surge to understand how the app was introduced and whether there was a genuine business need.
